Every Oracle Fusion finance team meets the same quiet deadline once a year. The new fiscal year arrives, and on the first morning the system either lets people work or it does not. Most rollover problems are not dramatic. They are small setup steps that slipped through in the rush, and they surface as a blocked invoice, a purchase order that fails its funds check, or a depreciation run that refuses to start. This guide walks through what to set up before the year turns, what the system handles on its own, and the traps that catch even experienced teams.

The work splits into three buckets: things you configure before the year starts, things that happen at the rollover itself, and things that belong to specific teams. Knowing which bucket each item sits in saves a lot of finger pointing in the first week of the year.

## Before the year starts

### Extend the accounting calendar
Add the new year's periods on every ledger, including the adjustment period, with names that follow the convention already in use. Do this early. Calendar options become hard to change once a period is opened or set to future enterable, so the structure needs to be right before anyone transacts in it.

### Extend the budget calendar
If budgetary control is in play, its calendar needs the new year too. This is a separate object from the accounting calendar, and it is easy to extend one and forget the other.

### Create the new sequence versions
There are two kinds, and both matter. The journal accounting and reporting sequences, the gapless statutory ones, need a new version for the new year. The document sequences for Payables and Receivables need the new year's sequences and their category assignments. Miss either and the first transaction of the year either fails or numbers itself wrong.

### Fixed Assets, the one most often left half done
This is where teams get caught. Three objects need the new year: the asset fiscal year, the depreciation calendar, and the prorate conventions. Two rules sit underneath them. First, if the book posts to the ledger, the depreciation calendar period names must match the general ledger period names. Second, and this is the real trap, Oracle stops depreciation once the current fiscal year is the last one you have defined. Adding only the upcoming year is therefore not enough once you are operating inside it. Keep the asset fiscal year at least one year ahead of the period you are depreciating. The cleanest habit is to add the next year every single year, in the same sitting, so you are always one ahead.

### Create and load the new year's control budgets
A budget calendar that carries the new year is not the same as a control budget for the new year. If budgetary control runs on requisitions and purchase orders, the new year needs its control budgets created and the amounts loaded. Without that, the first purchase order dated in the new year fails its funds check, and purchasing stops on day one. If you carry encumbrances, plan the year end carry forward of open commitments as part of the same exercise.

### Make cost and inventory periods available
Where costing and inventory are live, the new periods need to exist so the business can open them. Copying the general ledger periods into the cost calendar is the setup step. Opening the periods is a separate action that happens at the rollover.

## At the rollover

### Open the first period, and let the year end roll happen
When you open the first period of the new fiscal year, Oracle performs the year end close for you. It resets the income and expense accounts and moves the result into retained earnings, so there is no manual closing journal for that step. You open the first period of each ledger when you are ready to transact in it.

### Open each subledger on its own
Opening the general ledger period does not open the subledgers. Payables, Receivables, Assets, and Cash Management are each opened separately. This is the item teams most often miss, because the general ledger period was opened, the dashboard looked fine, and nobody checked the subledgers until a transaction would not post.

### Open the cost and inventory periods
A cost period can move to open only when the matching general ledger period is already open. Sequence the two correctly and this is a non event. Get them out of order and costing stalls.

## Owned by specific teams

The final depreciation of the closing year and the asset period close sit with the asset and finance users. Tax rate changes belong to the tax team and happen only when the authority issues them. The general ledger team usually owns the period opening. Name the owner for each of these before the year turns, because they are the items that stall when everyone assumes someone else has them in hand.

## The commonly missed list

A short list of what most often gets forgotten, drawn from real rollovers:

- The Fixed Assets fiscal year stops at the upcoming year, so depreciation fails the moment the team is operating inside it.
- The budget calendar is extended but the control budgets are not, so purchasing halts on the first working day.
- The journal sequence version or the document sequences for the new year are missing, so the first transactions fail or number themselves incorrectly.
- The subledger periods are forgotten because the general ledger period was opened and looked healthy.
- The withholding tax calendar, where period based withholding is used, is left without the new year's periods.

## Two habits worth keeping

First, verify rather than assume. Exporting the setup and reading it back, or checking the data directly, takes minutes and catches the silent gaps that a glance at the screen misses. For numbered sequences, a quick check that the issued numbers carry no duplicates is worth running every year.

Second, write it down. A simple checklist that records who owns each item, and whether it applies to this client, turns a yearly scramble into a half day of confirmation. Record the items that do not apply as well. That way next year nobody wastes an afternoon chasing a withholding tax calendar the client never used.

## Where this varies by client

Not every item applies everywhere, so treat the list as a superset and switch off what the client does not use, but switch it off on purpose. Project accounting periods only need separate attention when they are not kept common with the general ledger. Encumbrance carry forward only matters where encumbrance accounting is switched on. Revaluation and translation only apply where there is foreign currency to revalue. A separate asset tax book needs its own calendar only where such a book exists.

## The point of all this

The new year setup rewards teams that treat it as a small, deliberate routine rather than a last minute task. None of the steps are hard on their own. The cost almost always sits in the single one that was missed. Run the list, confirm each item, and the year turns without anyone noticing. That quiet is exactly the goal.
